Why you should try Linux

Intro - Why Linux

Chances are high that if you're browsing the Linux section of my site, you are a geeky(ish) person who likes to hang around the internet following geek or tech websites, forums and youtubes. In recent years, you might have noticed a couple of things happening:

Thing the First: Windows is getting objectively worse. Complaining about Windows (and Microsoft) has been a life-long hobby for many people - except this time it isn't the usual raging nerds and algorithm-driven whinge-fest. Unlike some of the other geek pile-ons you see online, you too can feel it, every time you fire up your PC. Hell, my father who can't tell the difference between Windows and Office and Facebook complains that it's turned to garbage. You feel it when Windows shows you an advert for OneDrive and you choose "later" because "Don't remind me" isn't an option. You feel it when your brand-new cutting-edge PC or laptop, with a kings-ransom in RAM and go-fast parts feels like it's shuffling along in prison irons.

Windows has genuinely become painful to use and more importantly, it's no longer 'yours'. Microsoft has systematically removed your ability to control your PC. Sure, you can still change the wallpaper and install some software, but Windows has ceased being a product you buy and has instead simply become another vector for driving subscriptions and harvesting that sweet-sweet user-data that tech companies are so badly addicted to.

You're not alone - we all feel it.


Image: Xbox Gamepass advert within Windows

More recently I've read the response from Microsoft - they claim to have heard the criticisim and are responding (arstechnica.com) by making several changes. Except Microsoft isn't stupid - they want your data, they need your data. They will get your data. Microsoft is like an abusive-ex; they don't really change, they simply dial it back to let the heat die down, waiting for everyone drop their guard again before they resume their shitty behaviour once more except this time you're a already a little worn down so resistance is harder. Microsoft is renown for their 'second attempts' - they rarely stumble twice in a row, particularly when it comes to Windows.

Thing the Second: Have you heard the news about our Lord and Savior Linux? There has been a significant increase in articles, videos and content pumping the alternative OS as a solid solution to the Windows problem. Perhaps you're Linux-curious or have tried it in the past. As a recent Linux migrant, I want to share what I've learnt about this recent trend.

Why now is the right time

Linux has come a very long way, even in the last 5 years. Maybe you too have looked at or tried Linux in the past and perhaps it didn't work for you. I would strongly recommend revisiting it again.

Personally, I'm a 30-year Windows veteran. I cut my teeth on a second-hand 286 with DOS 5.0 before building my IT career on Microsoft systems from Win95 forward which means I have a lot of habits and momentum in one direction to overcome. While I've dipped my toes into the Linux pond several times over the past 20-years, I could never commit to the move so it remained a novelty on secondary devices. At the time of writing, I've now been 100% Linux on my own devices for a year and I have never once looked back - so what's changed to turn this life-long MS-goon to the alternative offering?

Gaming has been brought in from the cold

For many average tech enthusiasts, gaming is the 'killer-app' that Linux needs in order to get mainstream attention - and I admit, it was always the one thing to bring me back to Microsoft's slimy embrace and prevented me from making the switch for my main PC. The origin of this recent change started in 2012 with the release of Windows 8. (Odd side-note, Win8.1 was my favourite versions of Windows - despite the universally-despised interface - I might discuss this later). This version also introduced the Microsoft Store which - like the Apple/Google App Stores - promised easy software installation for users while Microsoft took a cut of every purchase. The big-brains at Valve Software saw this and had a mild heart-attack. They had built their entire empire on building, and now selling games for Windows. If the Windows Store took hold, it would destroy their business. This drove Valve to develop a plan for a genuine alternative to the Windows monopoly.

Valve and Codeweavers have sunk what I imagine to be millions of dollars into developing the Proton compatibility layer. This allows many Windows games to work, both as well and sometimes better) than on Windows. Despite what I'd read, I was still pleasantly surprised at just how well it worked. I've played around a dozen of my favourite games to date, and all of them have worked with no special effort. Install Steam, install the game and play. The only 'issue' I've found so far was a graphical bug in Serious Sam 2 where the Siberian WereBull would appear as a giant graphical spike in the distance before finally rendering correctly at close-range, it was so minor I didn't bother looking for a fix. More surprising was how easy it was to play classic DOS games - even easier than it was in Windows!

It's Mabo, it's the vibe

In my own opinion, Linux of years past just looked and felt old. Perhaps this was due to the focus on the terminal for many users but for Normies like me, the GUI was lacking. Sure, it was fast, but KDE 4.x and Gnome 3 didn't have the polish we saw in Windows and MacOS at the time and were perhaps closer to Win98 than Vista or newer. This meant that Linux Desktops always felt several years behind the curve. Additionally, and this might sound strange but the movement of the mouse just felt wrong to me and annoyed me more than I ever imagined. No amount of tweaking seemed to fix this.


Image: KDE 4 Desktop: KDE4 felt like it was stuck in the early 'naughties'.

That vibe has improved significantly in the past few years! The modern Gnome and KDE environments are both excellent, modern Desktops that provide 90% of the modern Windows/MacOS polish and experience, but even with that missing 10%, since it leaves out all the undesirable features - subscription adverts, account sign-in and endless data tracking - that makes it an easy 120% greater than Win11 in my book.

As for the feel of the mouse, I don't know what changed but it just feels normal now. Even tweaking the settings for speed/acceleration, I can't 'break it' and make it feel like it used to so perhaps that was changed by a lower-level improvement rather than a default user-level setting.

Software

Software installation on Linux does not behave how most people coming from a Windows (or even MacOS) world have come to expect. On Windows or Mac, you download the binary (.exe file) or extract from a Zip file and run an install program. When you look at Linux, you see several options and need to decode what base Distro your OS is built on to ensure it will work. While most current Linux users probably have no issue with this, it does not translate to a great experience for average users who don't spend their time memorising the genealogy of their OS.

Arguably, Linux is easier than both Windows and Mac with the correct command to quickly install common software. In the GUI however, we see fragmentation that similarly reflects the fragmentation of the OS itself by requiring different installation methods between major versions - as we can see below with a screenshot of the download page of a popular application - VLC has nine different Linux options to choose from vs a single option for Windows (on desktop) and Mac.


Image: Options for VLC Media Player - note the several different options for Linux!

In recent years, new package management functions have gained mainstream acceptance in Linux and makes installation easier than ever for normal people. I won't go into the details of the benefits/drawbacks of how traditional Linux package management works but it can lead to problems when two applications share a dependency package and upgrading this package works fine for one app but breaks the other. Using the new package management tools like Flatpak, AppImage and Snap, users get an experience close to that of Windows where everything required by the app is included - at the cost of additional disk space as you likely end up with duplicates identical packages across several applications. It might not win favour with the most hard-core of Linux purest but it goes a long way to addressing the criticism from the highest levels of Linux back in 2014:


Video: Linus Torvalds - the creator of Linux - discusses why Linux has struggled on the desktop.

The Decline of Windows

As much as Linux has improved over the past few years, Microsoft has been hard at work, putting it's best effort into accelerating the transformation of Windows from a useful, easy to use OS into a data harvesting, advert platform. They cram CoPilot into everything they can, even if you don't want it, require you to setup and use a Microsoft account for your local PC which then signs into OneDrive and becomes your default location for saving files and documents. The old cynic in me sees this as building dependency - when the 'free' tier of the online storage is full, you are urged to upgrade your subscription to continue with the functionality you've now become used to or are even unaware you were using but are now committed and unskilled or too lazy to untangle yourself from. Apple has used similar tactics for years with iCloud.

Except that was the tactics of the old world - the world before AI. Subscriptions to online data storage are pennies, when Microsoft are thinking in dollars. The new cynic in me sees the real value is in using your data to train their AI models that they then sell to you - and more importantly, businesses. Every AI company swears that what they do is legit, but they willingly commit copyright infringement on a scale that makes movie piracy in the early 2000's appear quaint - and then have the lawyers and budget to make it perfectly legal after the fact. If they do these sort of things with the data owned by businesses with the money and desire to sue for unlawful use of their property, what do you think they're doing with your own data?

Beyond the conspiracy theories, have you used Windows 11 lately? As a home user it's awful, while in a enterprise environment, it's somewhat tolerable, since admin controls via Group Policy and such allows a business to control the more despicable aspects of Windows 11. Even then, on a fast PC, Windows 11 is slow. The start menu can take seconds to open, Windows Explorer takes several more if you're that lucky. Just opening folders can be an exercise in patience. File-search is nearly completely non-functional, while searching for an application to launch from the Start Menu is lottery at best. I used to launch everything from the start menu - hit the start key and start typing is infinitely faster than stopping to grab my mouse and trying to find an app to launch. I use Powershell once every six months or less these days, while I use Powerpoint weekly. If I enter 'power' into the start menu to launch Powerpoint, 6/10 times it will offer Powershell instead. It's not presenting results based on use, else it would default to Powerpoint, it's not presenting results based on alphabetical order else it would also present Powerpoint. Regardless, it should be consistent - yet it's not. Randomly I will get presented with Powerpoint and other times Powershell... then on other times I will get the power settings option.

It's at this point, Linux and the Open Source community becomes very attractive to anyone vaguely interested in technology and Microsoft's actions have helped increase the drive towards Linux. Any Distro of Linux you care to touch, it will be faster, less bloated and provide a greater user experience that the aggressively anti-user one that you currently receive via Windows.

Conclusion

Over the past 5 years, Linux has improved massively while Windows has become much worse. As mentioned by Linus in the video above, Linux has conquered every platform it has encountered except for the one it was designed for - the Desktop, except now, the tide is changing. Software packages are vastly improved, gaming is now possible, and the experience and options available on Linux are now world-class. I encourage anyone that hasn't tried Linux before or last tried it a few years ago to take a good look again because Linux is now better than ever! Take back control of your Personal Computer from Microsoft.
