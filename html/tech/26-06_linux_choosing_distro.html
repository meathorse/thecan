Linux: Choosing a Distro

How to try Linux without committment

You've read my other posts and somehow I've convinced you that Linux is worth a try - but you don't want to make any changes yet - you saw that giant image with 10,000 different Distros - how do you pick one from that mess? The good news is that for most people, "Linux" really only boils down to around a dozen options - still overwhelming if you think about it too much but much better than the family tree suggests.


Image: Distrosea.com lets you try different Distros and Desktop Environments right inside your browser!

First thing I recommend is to ignore the Distros and instead just try out the different Desktop Environments (DE) to find one that works for you. For most users, the Desktop Environment is the most significant choice to make when it comes to their PC - it's the 'way' the PC operates and how you go about launching programs, find your files and generally use the PC. The most well-known and popular are KDE (Windows-Like) and Gnome (MacOS-Like) but most Distros offer several different DE's as options. A very popular choice for recent Windows converts is Linux Mint which defaults to the Cinnamon DE. The great news is that you can try out all these options right now in your browser without installing anything:

DistroSea is a website that allows you to test a few-dozen different Linux Distros, and within each Distro, you get a range of DE's that are offered by that Distro. You can open each version and explore it as if it was installed on your PC to get a feel for how that DE operates. Just note that the demo's performance is slower that you would typically experience when installed on a PC.

If you're keen to give this a go, I recommend testing one Distro with several DE options that will give you a good feel for the difference between each desktop rather than worrying about why each Distro looks and feels different. Fedora is a good choice as it's a popular Distro and has options for all the most common DEs. The point to focus on during this test is to determine what you like the feel of when it comes to a PC - does it make sense to click on a 'start menu' or does having a dock across one edge of the page actually feel better? Do the icons and menus make sense, are they easy to read and navigate or does it look like visual clutter? All these factors are considerations. Even if you like the first one you try, run through a few options to be sure since Linux is like picking your nose - there is more than once choice to make... and like a good nose picking, it's a personal thing. Looking through a list of 'Best Desktop Environments for Linux' will only tell what they like, and not much about your preference.


Image: With DistroSea you can trial Fedora 44 in assorted Desktop Environments

What's next?

If you've found a Desktop Environment that you like, now you can continue to leverage DistroSea to find the actual Distro you like. A quick search online will give you several options for the most popular Distro with your choosen DE. Again, dive into the DistroSea and try out a handful of different Distros. This time you're looking a little wider. Each Distro will look and feel a little different as it likely has a unique combination of configuration and packages.

Wait, what!? A gaming Distro?

I've spent the past couple of posts talking about gaming on Linux being a significant driver for adoption and only now am I mentioned a "Gaming" Distro! Before you pull out the pitchforks, there is good reason for this. Firstly, this is a reward for reading this far, but also, any Distro can be a gaming Distro. In fact, the great work done by Valve has made it so easy, I game quite happily on Fedora without anything more special than installing Steam! Then you're probably now wondering why I even mentioned Bazzite at all?

Bazzite follows along with other gaming focused Distros by coming pre-loaded with Steam, Lutris and other gaming tools and enhancements that makes life just a little easier. The unique function that makes Bazzite a little different to most is that it is immutable. What this means is that the core of the Operating System can't be 'mutated' or changed (at least without a bit of work).

A traditional Distro will allow you to install packages and software however you want. When there are upgrades to packages, these are replaced as needed and anything not needed is left alone. Over time, your OS 'drifts' away from the original install state which can lead to problems (possible but unlikely in most cases). Bazzite (and other immutable Distros) use an 'image-based' update process. This means that when an upgrade happens, the entire OS is updated and replaced as a whole. There is no chance for the OS to 'drift' from the default configuration since it can't be changed. Additionally, if the upgrade fails for whatever reason, you can start up on the old configuration, a couple of quick commands and the updated image is removed and you're back on your previous working system. If this all sounds a bit too advanced for a 'beginner' Distro, you're wrong and I'll tell you why it's an excellent beginner-friendly option!

If you've every used a Windows PC at a Public Library or Internet Cafe, these likely had disk protection software installed that reset the system back to a known-state after each restart, removing any changes (and files) created or modified during use. Immutability is a very similar idea, except your personal files are kept in a separate space where they are not removed or wiped out when the OS is restarted or updated. Meanwhile, applications and their packages are also not installed traditionally, instead relying on Flatpak. The application and all its associated dependency packages are self-contained and unaffected by any other packages in the system or used by other applications.

All this means a few great things for new Linux users:
  • The system is always in a 'good' state and cannot drift over time
  • User changes made without understanding the consequences are unlikely to be 'fatal' to the OS - ask me how I know!
  • Flatpak is the most user-friendly way to install software on Linux and is closest to Windows installation since it is all self-contained

I believe that an immutable Distro + Flatpak is an excellent introduction to Linux for many users who want certainty and might be worried about breaking something since they've heard Linux can be tough. Especially anyone who has very simple/predictable use cases - email, web and a few productivity applications. However, nothing is ever perfect and while the immutable design provides great benefits, I'd be lying if I said this didn't introduce issues of its own.

The first is Flatpak which is still a relatively new function so not all software packages are available via Flatpak. This can be overcome by determining the primary software you require and ensuring it is all available in Flatpak format. However you may find as I did that over time you become more comfortable and perhaps you want to install a new application to achieve a goal - except it's not available as a Flatpak. There are ways around this but if you find yourself at the point where you're regularly working around the system to install traditional software packages, you may wish to jump to a traditional Distro - remember, they're free so you can always change your mind about what Distro you would like to run!

The second issue is that immutability is its own thing. A common thing in the Linux world is to lean on the 'core' Distro for support. Mint is based on Ubuntu and Ubuntu in turn is based on Debian - one of the oldest and most trusted Distros there is. In most cases, if you encounter a major issue, support articles and advice for Ubuntu and even Debian have a good chance of working for Linux Mint. As long as you're searching for support within the correct branch of the family tree, you'll probably be ok. The incident I alluded to earlier was my own fault. Bazzite is a relatively new Distro which means that support can be limited - however, it is based on Fedora so taking this literally as I have done in the past, I looked to Fedora support pages to achieve what I needed to do. One system-configuration change later and a restart but- oops, the system no longer starts up! I had not understood the change I made to a core system file and I broke my OS. However what has to be one of the easiest recoveries of my career followed as I found the commands required - the first told my PC to load the last good state. This gave me my system back (desktop and all) to the state it was before I made the change. The second command promoted this known-good state back to my default option. Crisis over!

Summary

Ultimately, the choice is so vast when it comes to Linux, it's best just to make the best choice you can and then see where it takes you. The beauty of this OS is that it's free - and you're free to choose a new Distro every day if you like.
